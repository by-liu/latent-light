A good consumer AI product does more than produce an impressive answer. It gives
someone a reason to return: getting better at a skill, finishing a creative
project, enjoying a relationship or taking a recurring chore off their mind.

This landscape follows **36 company groups across ten product categories**.
It includes broad assistants from large ecosystems and model providers alongside
specialist products. Related products share a row. Some are consumer-led; others combine personal
use with professional, school, enterprise or API businesses. The directory is
a discovery tool, not an investment ranking or a list of equally mature products.

**Three things to notice:** the interface often matters as much as the model;
repeat use needs a real purpose; and revenue, engagement and user benefit are
different kinds of evidence. Those are the questions guiding the analysis below.

## The need comes before the modality

A voice interface may teach, entertain or coordinate a household. An image model
may serve a hobbyist or a production studio. [Figure 1](#figure-1) groups products
by the reason someone uses them, with proposed tests of value rather than
assumed retention advantages.

| Lens | What it does | A useful test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Learn** | Practice a skill with feedback and progression. | Can the user do something better without the app? |
| **Create** | Turn an idea into something worth editing and sharing. | How many outputs become finished work? |
| **Connect** | Enjoy an ongoing conversational relationship. | Does the experience support life beyond the conversation? |
| **Understand** | Transform material into a useful explanation or personal work. | Is the result faithful, and does understanding improve? |
| **Organize** | Reduce the work of coordinating household life. | Is less checking and correction needed overall? |
| **Decide** | Explore choices that fit preferences and constraints. | Does the final choice serve the user, not only the seller? |

Categories overlap. Placement describes the product pattern, not market share or measured quality.

## Product patterns worth studying

### Broad assistants and specialists: reach versus depth

ChatGPT and Claude span many tasks; Gemini and Copilot connect personal help
to existing apps and documents. Meta AI, Apple Intelligence and Alexa+ put
assistance into social, device and home contexts. Qwen App connects conversation
to commerce and services.[^broad-chatgpt][^broad-claude][^broad-gemini][^broad-copilot][^broad-meta][^broad-apple][^broad-alexa][^broad-qwen]

**My interpretation:** ecosystems can reduce distribution and integration friction.
A specialist still has to earn a place through its curriculum, creative controls,
community or completed workflow. Neither a large installed base nor a focused
interface proves a better outcome. Compare the work remaining after the first answer.

### Speak and Praktika: practice, not just conversation

Speak organizes spoken practice around a curriculum and feedback. Praktika uses
avatar tutors and adaptive conversations. Both also have business channels.
Their difference is not simply which model answers the student; it is how the
experience chooses the next exercise, corrects mistakes and supports another
attempt.[^dir-speak-product][^dir-praktika-product][^dir-praktika-business]

**The product lesson:** lowering the embarrassment and effort of practice can
be valuable, but conversation volume is not proof of learning. The stronger test
is whether a learner can communicate better without the app, with a comparison
that accounts for their starting ability and practice time.

Synthesis Tutor adds authored mathematics pedagogy; StudyFetch starts with the
learner’s own course materials and study deadlines. These are different sources
of structure, not interchangeable tutoring wrappers.[^dir-synthesis][^dir-studyfetch]

### Suno and Krea: a first draft is only the beginning

Suno combines music generation with editing and studio tools. Krea’s realtime
canvas makes generation part of an iterative visual interaction. Both serve
personal expression and professional creation.[^suno][^krea]

**The product lesson:** look beyond the quality of one generation. Can someone
control the result, keep a promising part, fix a weak part and finish something
they want to use? Useful measures include editing effort, successful exports
and finished projects. A gallery of attractive outputs does not answer those
questions.

Tripo’s 3D assets and Viggle’s character animation show why “creation” is still
too broad for a direct comparison. A printable object, a production asset and
a joke shared with friends have different standards of success.[^dir-tripo-product][^dir-viggle]

### Tolan, Character.AI and Sekai: relationship versus participation

Tolan designs an ongoing relationship with a nonhuman character. Character.AI
centers character conversations and a creator community. Sekai lets people
create, play and remix interactive mini-apps. Continuity can come from a character,
a community or a shared creation, not only a chat history.[^tolan][^dir-character][^dir-sekai]

**The product lesson:** distinguish the person who creates from the person who
returns to consume or play. Sekai’s June 2026 announcement reports 15 million
mini-apps created; that is an output count, not 15 million retained users.[^dir-sekai]
For companion products, satisfaction and healthy boundaries need assessment
separate from time spent in conversation.

### Rosebud and Endel: a ritual is different from a feed

Rosebud combines journaling, reflection and memory. Endel adapts soundscapes
to context rather than offering LLM conversation. Both suggest a recurring
ritual, but their mechanisms and intended outcomes differ.[^dir-rosebud-round][^dir-endel]

**The product lesson:** ask what the person values after the session. Rosebud’s
fundraising announcement includes a customer survey and engagement figures;
those do not establish clinical efficacy or an independently measured causal
benefit.[^dir-rosebud-round] Product positioning should not silently become
medical evidence.

### Ohai, Mindtrip and Daydream: useful help has a completion boundary

Ohai turns household information into schedules and tasks, including human
support for difficult inputs. Mindtrip includes flight and hotel booking
experiences. Daydream helps with fashion discovery while purchases flow through
partners.[^ohai][^dir-mindtrip-flights][^dir-mindtrip-stays][^daydream]

**The product lesson:** a recommendation, a prepared plan and a completed
transaction are different promises. Measure the work remaining for the person,
including corrections, checkout and recovery. Shopping and travel also make
commercial incentives visible: a useful assistant should respect the user’s
constraints, not merely increase conversion for a seller.

## Business evidence

Funding shows that capital was committed, not that a product retains users or
earns a profit. A free shopper-facing service, a paid learning subscription,
an inference-credit product and a hybrid concierge need different economic
models. Several entries also sell APIs or institutional offerings; company-wide
revenue should not automatically be attributed to consumer use.

RevenueCat’s 2026 report, targeting 2025 performance, provides a useful
counterweight to enthusiasm. Among qualifying apps using its platform, the AI
segment has higher median realized revenue per payer after a year, but lower
paid-subscription retention on annual plans.[^revenuecat]

| Reported measure | AI apps | Non-AI apps |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Median realized revenue per payer after one year | \$30.16 | \$21.37 |
| Paid subscriptions retained after 12 months, annual plans | 21.1% | 30.7% |

Source: RevenueCat. These are different aggregate subscription measures, not
usage retention, app-specific results or a causal test of AI. The sample is
platform-selected and subject to inclusion thresholds.[^revenuecat]

My proposed economic lens is **cost per useful outcome**. Include generation,
retries, review, support and delivery. For a hybrid service, human handling is
part of the cost. For creative products, usage rights and usable exports matter.
For companions, user welfare remains a separate question from monetization.

## What could remain valuable as models improve?

The examples suggest four candidates: a workflow that removes effort, domain
structure that guides progress, context that genuinely improves the next session,
and a community or distribution channel people already value. None is a proven
moat merely because a product page mentions it.

To study one company more deeply, ask:

- What recurring need survives after the novelty wears off?
- Which part of the experience is more useful than a general assistant?
- What evidence connects use to an outcome the person actually wants?
- Can the person leave with their work and useful history?
- What happens when the system is wrong, unavailable or discontinued?
