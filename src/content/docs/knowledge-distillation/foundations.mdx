A teacher can tell a student more than the winning answer. It can reveal which
alternatives are plausible and how strongly it prefers one to another. That
extra structure is the intuition behind soft-target distillation.[^hinton]

This page develops the objectives before considering complete training
recipes. It assumes familiarity with next-token prediction; the
[Transformer foundations](/architectures/foundations/) provide that background.
The equations below describe explicit mathematical settings, not one recipe
shared by every model.

## From a label to a distribution

Suppose a classifier chooses between cat, dog, fox and car. A hard label for
cat is the vector `(1, 0, 0, 0)`. It does not say whether dog is a more plausible
alternative than car. A teacher distribution can express that distinction.
These toy categories are an illustration, not a claim about a particular model.
[Figure 1](#figure-1) makes the additional information visible.

For logit vector <MathExpression tex="z" /> and positive temperature
<MathExpression tex="\tau" />, define:

<MathExpression display number="1" tex="p_i^{(\tau)}=\frac{\exp(z_i/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{V}\exp(z_j/\tau)}" />

Here, <MathExpression tex="V" /> is the number of classes or vocabulary entries.
For fixed finite logits, increasing the temperature softens the distribution
toward uniform; lowering it concentrates mass on the largest logits.
This training temperature is a separate choice from deployment sampling.[^hinton]

The relative probabilities sometimes called **dark knowledge** are not hidden
facts that the student automatically acquires. They are information in the
teacher's predictive behavior. They can contain useful structure, uncertainty,
or systematic errors.

## Match probabilities, not parameter files

Let <MathExpression tex="p" /> be a frozen teacher distribution and
<MathExpression tex="q_\theta" /> the student's distribution at the same input.
Forward KL is:

<MathExpression display number="2" tex="D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q_\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{V}p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_{\theta,i}}" />

Assume both distributions use the same events and the student assigns positive
probability wherever the teacher does. Expanding the definition gives this
local derivation:

<MathExpression display number="3" tex="\begin{aligned}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q_\theta)&=H(p,q_\theta)-H(p),\\ H(p,q_\theta)&=-\sum_i p_i\log q_{\theta,i}.\end{aligned}" />

The teacher entropy <MathExpression tex="H(p)" /> is constant with respect to
the student. Minimizing soft-target cross-entropy therefore gives the same
student gradient as forward KL in this fixed-target setting. A hard label is
the special case where the target puts all mass on one event.

A common classical objective combines real labels with softened supervision:

<MathExpression display number="4" tex="\mathcal L=(1-\alpha)\mathcal L_{\mathrm{hard}}+\alpha\tau^2D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p^{(\tau)}\Vert q_\theta^{(\tau)}\right)" />

Here, <MathExpression tex="\alpha\in[0,1]" /> controls the mixture and the hard
loss uses temperature 1. The conventional
<MathExpression tex="\tau^2" /> factor compensates for the approximate
temperature-related gradient scaling described in the original formulation.
It does not make every temperature or modern objective equivalent.[^hinton]

## Why the KL direction matters

Reverse KL swaps the distributions:

<MathExpression display number="5" tex="D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\theta\Vert p)=\sum_i q_{\theta,i}\log\frac{q_{\theta,i}}{p_i}" />

The weighting changes the question. Forward KL weights discrepancies by what
the **teacher** considers likely. Reverse KL weights them by what the
**student** currently considers likely. Unlike the fixed teacher entropy in
Equation (3), the student entropy in reverse KL depends on the parameters.
Dropping it changes the objective.

An exact toy calculation makes the asymmetry concrete:

| Candidate student | Probabilities over three events | Forward KL | Reverse KL |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broad allocation | `(0.45, 0.35, 0.20)` | 0.225 nats | 0.466 nats |
| Concentrated allocation | `(0.98, 0.01, 0.01)` | 0.741 nats | 0.296 nats |

The teacher here is `(0.70, 0.29, 0.01)`. Values are calculated from Equations
(2) and (5), rounded to three decimals. Among these **two candidates**, forward
KL prefers the broad one and reverse KL prefers the concentrated one. With no
capacity restriction, both are minimized by matching the teacher exactly.
The table is not a proof that every neural student follows these patterns.

MiniLLM uses reverse KL to address limited student capacity in generative
language modeling, with policy-gradient optimization, variance reduction,
teacher-mixed sampling and length normalization. Its gains belong to that
tested recipe, not a theorem that reverse KL always wins.[^minillm]

## Language models introduce a second distribution

A language model predicts complete responses through next-token probabilities.
For prompt <MathExpression tex="x" /> and response
<MathExpression tex="y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)" />:

<MathExpression display number="6" tex="p(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{n}p(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})" />

There are now two choices: **which prefix is visited**, and **which next-token
distribution is matched there**. A teacher-generated response supplies teacher
prefixes. A student rollout supplies student prefixes. Scoring a sequence with
teacher forcing does not mean the teacher generated that sequence.

For a shared vocabulary, finite response horizon including stopping, and the
usual support assumptions, the chain rule gives:

<MathExpression display number="7" tex="D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)=\mathbb E_{y\sim P}\left[\sum_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p(\cdot\mid x,y_{\lt t})\Vert q(\cdot\mid x,y_{\lt t})\right)\right]" />

Capital letters denote sequence distributions. The expectation in this
identity is over **teacher trajectories**. Evaluating forward token KL along
student trajectories is a valid training objective, but is not automatically
the same sequence-level forward KL. Reversing the sequence KL instead induces
an expectation over student trajectories. Length averaging or truncated
vocabularies changes the exact formulation.[^gkd][^minillm]

Sequence distillation often replaces the full expectation with selected teacher
responses and trains their token likelihood. Kim and Rush's translation work
uses high-probability teacher sequences, including a beam-search approximation;
that selection is not the entire teacher distribution.[^seqkd]

## What can limit transfer?

**Capacity and learnability.** A stronger teacher need not be easier to imitate.
Teacher Assistant KD studies intermediate students to bridge large capacity
gaps in its classification experiments. This motivates testing teacher–student
fit, not adopting a universal assistant-size formula for LLMs.[^takd]

**Coverage.** Matching the teacher on a narrow set of prefixes constrains the
student there. It does not establish behavior on an unseen document type,
language or tool state. The student also brings its own pretraining and
architecture; distillation gains should be measured relative to that starting
point.

**Teacher quality.** The objective encourages agreement, not truth by
definition. Correct labels, executable checks and independent evaluation can
anchor the recipe when teacher confidence is misleading.

**Knowledge versus execution.** A recent controlled preprint separates random
factual associations from composing known facts. Its reverse-KL experiments
transfer composition more effectively than held-out facts; forward KL restores
more factual transfer. That is a setting-specific finding, not evidence that
all on-policy distillation cannot teach facts.[^knowledge-skill]
