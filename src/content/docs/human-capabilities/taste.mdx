You have twenty possible designs, three polished drafts, and an AI tool ready
to produce more. Which version should you keep? What should you change?
What would make the result worth someone's attention?

For this guide, **taste is developed discernment expressed in what you notice,
choose, and shape**. It involves recognizing quality in a particular domain
and making choices that fit a purpose, an audience, and a point of view.
This working definition combines ideas about making and evaluative judgment;
it is a synthesis, rather than a single established psychological trait.[^graham][^evaluative]

The opportunity with AI is to direct additional capability toward work you
consider worthwhile. The challenge is that generating something impressive,
recognizing its quality, and choosing what to develop are different activities.
Taste helps you examine those choices. It offers no guarantee of success or
protection from changes in employment.

## What we mean by taste

The word carries several meanings. “I like it” reports a preference.
“This works well for these readers” makes a judgment that needs reasons.
“I would change this opening” turns a judgment into a making decision.
They can inform one another, but they answer different questions.

| Meaning | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Personal preference | What attracts, moves or interests you: a restrained design, an exuberant one, a particular kind of story. |
| Aesthetic appreciation | How form, rhythm, composition, surprise or meaning contribute to your experience. |
| Evaluative judgment | How well work meets relevant standards and intentions, including the quality of your own work.[^evaluative] |
| Taste in making | Which possibility to pursue, what to retain, and how to refine the whole. This is the guide's practical emphasis. |

An editor may admire an unusual sentence while removing it because it disrupts
the passage. A designer can appreciate an elaborate poster and choose a simpler
invitation for someone scanning a community noticeboard. These are original
illustrations of judging work in context. Having a preference does not require
winning an argument about universal superiority.

### An old problem in a new conversation

Taste is visible in 2026 technology coverage.[^current] Anthropic's 2026 trends
report also describes engineers retaining design choices that require context
or taste. It is a commercial report containing predictions and self-described
practice, not an independent experiment proving taste's economic value.
These sources establish attention in that community, not a measured growth
rate in public interest.[^trends]

The underlying question is much older. In *Of the Standard of Taste*, David
Hume argued that practice, comparison and attention free from prejudice can
improve criticism. He also acknowledged differences in temperament and context
that need not be faults. This is a philosophical argument, with historical
assumptions about who qualifies as a critic; it is not experimental proof of
one universal standard.[^hume]

Paul Graham's 2002 *Taste for Makers* approaches the problem through creating
things. He argues that taste can improve as makers gain knowledge, recognize
weaknesses, and redesign their work. It is a useful practitioner perspective,
whose proposed principles should be examined in a particular domain.[^graham]

## Shared standards and personal differences

There are choices we can discuss against a stated aim: whether someone can
find an event's time, whether a claim is supported, or whether a paragraph
explains the idea it promises. There are also choices for which informed people
can prefer different rhythms, forms, or emotional effects. A useful approach
is to make the relevant standard explicit before treating disagreement as a
mistake. This distinction is a practical interpretation of evaluative judgment
and research on aesthetic preferences.[^evaluative][^shared]

Vessel and colleagues compared preferences across visual domains in two
experiments. Participants agreed more about faces and natural landscapes than
about artworks and architecture. The second experiment compared landscapes
with exterior architecture within the same people, finding a difference in
agreement without a difference in rating reliability. A person can have a
repeatable preference that others do not share. The study does not establish
standards for every culture, medium or professional task.[^shared]

what the workshop offers and find the practical details quickly**. Which
version would you choose, and what feature supports your choice?

The useful move is to name a feature, connect it to an intention, and recognize
its tradeoff. “The activity is clear in the headline” gives a collaborator
something to examine. “It has better taste” leaves the reasoning hidden.

## How a judgment forms

### You learn what to notice

Perceptual learning research studies changes in the information people extract
from experience. Kellman and Massey distinguish discovering relevant structure
from becoming faster and more fluent at recognizing it. Their review includes
specific learning tasks, such as mathematical representations and visual
classification. It does not directly test a general programme for creative taste.[^perception]

A practical extrapolation is to practise noticing relationships within one
domain: the sequence of ideas in an explanation, the relative prominence of
information in a layout, or how a product's details support its main use.
Rather than collecting admired examples without examining them, ask what you
can now distinguish that you previously missed.

Some recognition can become quick without being easy to explain. That does not
make every quick reaction expert. Kahneman and Klein argue that reliable
intuition needs an environment with learnable regularities and an opportunity
to learn them; subjective confidence alone does not establish accuracy.
Applied here, confidence in choosing a poster is a reason to examine your
experience and feedback, not a credential that settles the choice.[^intuition]

### Feeling and appraisal can separate

Leder and colleagues' model of aesthetic appreciation includes perception,
classification, interpretation and evaluation. It distinguishes aesthetic
emotion from aesthetic judgment. The model helps explain why understanding
what a work is attempting can contribute to appreciation. It is a theoretical
account of aesthetic processing, not a complete settled explanation of taste.[^aesthetics]

For example, you might find a work uncomfortable while admiring how precisely
it creates that experience. You might enjoy a familiar style while recognizing
that it does little for the task at hand. These examples illustrate a distinction
between your response and your appraisal; neither response needs to be concealed.

Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman's processing fluency account links ease of
processing with aesthetic pleasure. Their review considers repetition, contrast,
symmetry and other influences, while noting roles for expectations and how
people attribute the experience. This helps explain some immediate appeal;
it does not establish that familiar, symmetrical or simple work is always best.[^fluency]

A practical implication is to look again after the first impression. Ask what
remains interesting, what becomes clearer with attention, and whether a
pleasant surface supports the work's purpose.

### Social context enters the choice

In *Distinction*, Pierre Bourdieu treats taste as acquired ways of classifying
and appreciating, shaped by social position and socialization. The inspected
conclusion offers a historical sociological account of how preferences can
express membership and distinction. It should not become a claim that every
choice is explained by class or that one group's taste is inherently superior.[^bourdieu]

Experimental work also shows that appraisal is sensitive to context. Salganik,
Dodds and Watts created an online music market with 14,341 participants and
48 unfamiliar songs. Randomly assigned participants either saw previous
download choices or chose independently. Stronger social influence increased
inequality and unpredictability in downloads. Their quality proxy was popularity
in the independent condition, not objective musical merit. Market success
therefore cannot simply be read as a verdict on artistic quality.[^social]

Bellaiche and colleagues randomly labeled AI generated paintings as human or
AI created. Across two studies, the human label produced higher ratings.
The manipulation concerns beliefs about origin, not an actual comparison of
human and AI creation. Origin can itself be a legitimate part of what people
value; the finding shows that judgments involve more than visible form.[^labels]

A useful self-check is to state both the object and the context you are judging:
“I like this composition” and “I value how it was made” are claims you can
examine separately. Removing names or popularity counts for an initial comparison
can help you investigate your reasons, although this exercise is a proposal,
not a proven way to eliminate bias.

## Why taste matters in the AI era

The strongest case is about **directing, selecting and refining possibilities**.
AI can assist with producing work, yet a productive workflow still needs an
account of what the work is for and how to assess it. This is our interpretation
of the evidence below; these studies do not measure a single “taste advantage”
that transfers to every task.

### Better output and better selection are different

In the published Procter & Gamble experiment, 791 professionals completed a
one day product innovation workshop with or without GPT‑4 assistance, alone
or in pairs. All conditions generated five initial ideas before selecting one.
AI improved the quality of generated ideas, but did not improve selection of
the highest rated idea. Teams without AI selected their best idea approximately
50% of the time, compared with roughly 37% in the AI conditions.[^teammate]

Selected ideas with AI were still better in absolute quality:
the improved set of options compensated for the weaker selection pattern.
The experiment does not establish that AI makes all human judgment worse,
or isolate why selection differed. It shows why output quality and selection
ability should be evaluated separately.[^teammate]

For your own project, compare whether AI improves the available drafts with
whether you can recognize which draft serves the brief. Those can call for
different improvements: more useful alternatives, clearer criteria, a second
reviewer, or feedback from the people the work is meant to help.

### More polish can coexist with less diversity

Doshi and Hauser's experiment involved 293 writers producing eight sentence
stories and 600 separate evaluators. Writers could receive GPT‑4 starting
ideas, or write without that assistance. Access to AI ideas improved ratings
of creativity, writing and enjoyment, particularly for writers with lower
initial creativity scores. Yet assisted stories became more similar to one
another. These results concern a constrained writing task, not all art or
every current model.[^creativity]

One practical response is to explore several directions before committing.
Bring your own observations, constraints and references into the work. Compare
what distinguishes the alternatives as well as what makes each acceptable.
This is a proposed response to the diversity finding, not a tested recipe
that guarantees originality.

### Make the standard part of the collaboration

“Make it better” leaves much unspecified. A more useful brief might identify
who will encounter the work, what they should understand, what tone fits,
and what must be accurate. AI can help generate contrasting options or articulate
possible weaknesses. You still need to decide whether those criticisms matter
for your project and test important assumptions with appropriate evidence.

For a factual explanation, clarity and style need to accompany correctness.
For an invitation, visual coherence needs to accompany accurate details.
For a product, the experience needs to serve the people using it. These are
proposed ways to make judgment discussable, rather than a universal quality formula.

## Can AI learn taste?

Some evaluative preferences can be modeled, and some tasks remain difficult.
Two 2026 investigations show why the answer depends on **what is judged,
whose preferences supply the labels, and what counts as success**.

In the August version of *AI Can Learn Scientific Taste*, Tong and colleagues
train models using community signals, including citations, to learn preferences
over scientific work. They report improvements against strong model baselines
and transfer to later papers and other evaluations. Citations are imperfect
proxies for scientific value. Their generated ideas are mainly judged by other
models and are not experimentally validated. This is a preprint about bounded
scientific evaluation, not evidence of universal aesthetic discernment.[^scientific]

Anthropic's August TASTE evaluation asks models to choose between AI safety
research proposals using experienced researchers' preferences as labels.
Its best tested model, Fable 5, scored 60%, compared with an estimated 77%
human agreement. The benchmark contains 92 filtered pairs drawn from 50
proposals, with ten researchers contributing judgments. Filtering for confident
agreement and repeated proposals limits generalization. Agreement with those
labels is not the same as discovering an objective best research direction.[^aitaste]

These results use different tasks and metrics, so their scores should not be
combined or compared as a ranking of general taste. They give reasons to
investigate what can be learned and delegated, while retaining uncertainty.
They do not support a claim that taste will remain permanently exclusive to
humans. The human task includes deciding the aims and standards of a particular
project, including values that an evaluation metric might omit.

## How to develop your taste

The practices below are proposals informed by the research. The assembled
approach has not been validated as a programme that improves a general taste
score. Pick one domain and a real piece of work so that the choices have
something concrete to answer to.

### 1. Write the brief before collecting options

Name the intended audience, the purpose, and a few relevant constraints.
For a workshop invitation: “Help a neighbour decide whether to attend, show
what happens, and make the practical details easy to find.” For a paragraph:
“Help a newcomer explain the central idea accurately.”

Add a point of view when appropriate: warm, playful, restrained, unsettling,
or exploratory. Keep it connected to the purpose. A brief gives you reasons
for choices without requiring every project to have the same style.

### 2. Build a small library you can explain

Choose a few examples from your domain, including work outside your usual
preferences. Record a feature, its effect, and a tradeoff: “The opening gives
me a concrete situation before introducing the concept, but postpones the
formal definition.” Return to the whole work so that you see how the parts fit.

Educational research on evaluative judgment proposes using exemplars,
comparison and commentary to develop an understanding of quality. An admired
example is more useful when you examine its decisions; merely collecting it
does not show what you have learned.[^evaluative]

Include work you initially misunderstood and examples that suit a different
brief. The aim is to increase what you can notice and discuss, while leaving
room for your preferences to change.

### 3. Compare before reading someone else's verdict

Put two alternatives against the same brief. Write your initial preference,
a specific reason, and your uncertainty. Then read another person's analysis
or ask for feedback. Notice whether it reveals something you missed or simply
values a different outcome.

Try hiding author names, AI labels or popularity signals during a first pass,
then restore them and examine what changes. The label and social-influence
studies motivate that experiment; they do not prove that blind comparison is
always the right evaluation method.[^labels][^social]

### 4. Turn a criticism into a revision

Replace a vague verdict with a diagnosis you can act on. “This feels generic”
might become “The invitation gives an ambitious promise but does not say what
we will actually do.” Then make one change that addresses the diagnosis.

Compare the revision with the brief and the whole piece. A sentence can improve
on its own while weakening the rhythm of the surrounding passage. A detail
can become more prominent while competing with another important detail.
Making exposes those tradeoffs in a way that an abstract verdict leaves hidden.

### 5. Seek feedback that can teach you something

Ask a knowledgeable person what they notice, and ask an intended reader or
user what they understand or experience. These people can provide different
kinds of information. “Which sentence made the promise unclear?” or “What do
you expect to do at the workshop?” gives you more to examine than a request
for a general score.

A meta analysis of 54 experimental and quasi-experimental peer assessment
studies found a small to moderate average benefit to academic performance.
The studies often combined giving and receiving feedback, so they do not
isolate which activity caused the benefit. Outcomes and methods also varied.
This supports examining critique as a learning activity, while leaving its
transfer to broad creative taste uncertain.[^peer]

Keep a record of what you expected and what feedback revealed. Look for
recurring mistakes and improvements within the same domain. Reliable learning
needs opportunities to discover whether your judgment was useful; confidence
and accumulated time alone do not establish that.[^intuition]

### 6. Use AI as a participant in evaluation

Ask for alternatives that differ in a named way, or for critiques against
specified criteria. Make an initial judgment yourself before comparing it
with the tool's analysis. Require reasons that refer to the actual work.
An illustrative instruction is:

> Compare these versions for a neighbour considering a beginner workshop.
> Check clarity of the activity, accuracy of the details, and tone.
> Give a reason tied to each version. Identify a tradeoff and an uncertainty.
> Suggest one revision that preserves what is working.

This is a proposed prompt, not a validated training method. Examine the
critique as carefully as the draft: a confident explanation can rely on the
wrong assumption or prefer a style that does not serve your audience.
Use appropriate factual checks, specialist review or audience feedback for
important decisions. AI assisted generation and selection remain separate
things to evaluate.[^teammate][^scientific]

## A small practice experiment

Choose one real artifact, such as an invitation, an explanation or a product
screen. The sequence below is an original proposal, not an experimentally
tested taste programme or a promise of rapid transformation.

| Session | Practice and record |
| --- | --- |
| Define and notice | Write a brief. Study three relevant examples. Record a feature, a reason and a tradeoff for each. |
| Compare and commit | Produce or collect two alternatives. Choose one before reading outside commentary. Record your reason and uncertainty. |
| Revise and check | Make a specific revision. Obtain informed critique or feedback from an intended reader. Record what matched or challenged your expectation. |
| Revisit | Review the whole artifact against the brief. Decide what to retain, change or investigate next. Keep the earlier version so the difference is visible. |

Look for concrete changes: you can identify a problem more precisely, explain
a choice, revise a weak passage, or anticipate a reader's misunderstanding.
These are informal observations of progress, not a validated measure of taste.
You may also discover a genuine preference difference that needs negotiation
rather than correction.

## Taste and agency work together

[High agency](/human-capabilities/high-agency/) emphasizes choosing a direction,
taking a bounded step, and learning from what happens. Taste concerns the
quality and character of the choices you make along that path. One can give
the other something to work with: making creates material to judge, and
judging helps you decide what to make next. This is a practical connection
between the two guides, not a demonstrated multiplication of personality traits.

The aim is to become more attentive, more capable of explaining and revising
your choices, and more responsive to what the work is for. Keep your own
preferences available for examination, and give other people room to value
different things. A considered point of view can coexist with curiosity.
